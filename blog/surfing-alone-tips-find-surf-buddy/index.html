Surfing Alone: When to Go and How to Enjoy It Safely

By L'équipe Foamies · 8 November 2026

Surfing alone can be pure joy when you feel comfortable, know the break and have other surfers in the water. When to go, good habits to keep, and how to find a surf buddy if you want one.

Surfing alone can be pure joy: no schedule to coordinate, your own rhythm, just you, the waves and your thoughts. Plenty of surfers love it. Can surfing alone be dangerous? It could be if you go unprepared, so save solo sessions for when you feel comfortable, at a break you know, with at least a few other surfers in the water.

Why surfers love going solo

A solo session is often a little escape. You switch off from everyday life, you wait for nobody, and there is nothing to prove.

You also get to decide everything: when you go, where, and for how long. Sneak in an early surf before work, stay ten more minutes for one last wave, or head in whenever you feel like it. With no chatting between sets (the groups of waves that arrive together), you tune in more to the ocean and to how your surfing feels.

None of that means you can't love surfing with friends too. The two go really well together.

When to surf alone: three things to check

To keep it fun, make sure these three boxes are ticked before you paddle out:

  • You feel comfortable. You are a good swimmer, you could get back to shore without your board, and today's conditions suit your level. If you hesitate when you look at the water, trust that feeling: there will be other days.
  • You know the break. You know where to paddle out, where to come in and how the spot changes with the tide. For a spot that is new to you, wait until you have surfed it with someone.
  • There are other surfers out. You don't need to know them. You are on your own without being isolated: someone can see you, and you can keep an eye out for others too.

If you are a beginner, enjoy company for a little longer. You will learn to read currents and waves, and you can head out on your own when you feel ready.

Is surfing alone dangerous? What could go wrong

Most solo sessions go perfectly well. It still helps to know what could complicate things, so you can plan for it calmly:

  • A current pulling you out to sea or along the beach.
  • Fatigue, when a session runs longer than you planned.
  • Cold, which tires you out faster than you would think (which is why a wetsuit matched to the water temperature helps).
  • A knock from your board, or rocks and reef close to the surface at low tide.
  • A snapped leash (the cord between your board and your ankle), which means a swim back to shore.

These are small problems that are usually easy to handle. The one real difference when you are alone is that no friend is there to notice something is off. That is exactly why it is nice to have other surfers around.

Good habits before you paddle out

A few simple habits that take two minutes:

  1. Tell someone which spot you are going to and when you expect to be out. A text is enough.
  2. Check the forecast: swell size, wind, tide. If it is beyond your level, save it for another day.
  3. Watch the water for a few minutes from the beach, to see where waves are breaking, where others paddle out and where the currents are.
  4. Check your gear, especially your leash.
  5. Come in before you are exhausted. The best last wave is one you catch with energy left.
  6. Be friendly in the lineup (the area where surfers wait for waves): a quick hello and following the surf etiquette and priority rules go a long way.

If a rip current catches you

This is worth knowing whether you are alone or not. On many beaches, rip currents (channels of water flowing back out to sea) can look like a calm gap with no waves.

If you get caught in one, stay calm:

  • Don't fight it by paddling straight at the beach, you would wear yourself out for nothing.
  • Stay with your board: it floats, and it is your best friend here.
  • Paddle parallel to the shore to get out of the current, then head in where waves are breaking.
  • If you can't get out, raise one arm and wave it to ask for help.

To learn where rips form at a particular spot, ask the lifeguards or a local surf school. And if you see someone else in trouble, alert the lifeguards or call emergency services (112 works across Europe, otherwise use your local number). If you can help without putting yourself at risk, push your board to them.

Want company? How to find surf buddies

Even if you love solo sessions, it is great to have a crew for exploring new spots, bigger days (common in winter, see our guide to winter surfing in Europe), or just sharing a good surf. And if you are learning to surf, it is also the most fun way to improve. A few ideas:

  • Group lessons at a surf school (or a surf camp, depending on what you need): you meet people at your level who want to keep going after the course.
  • Surf clubs, which organise sessions near you.
  • Local surf shops, where you often bump into locals.
  • The lineup itself: chatting between sets works more often than you would think.
  • Apps: Foamies Club, for example, is a free app to meet surfers near you and set up sessions together.

When you arrange a session with someone new, talk about your levels so you can pick a spot you will both enjoy.

FAQ

Is it dangerous to surf alone?

Not necessarily. It could be at an unfamiliar spot, in conditions above your level, or with nobody around. When you feel comfortable, know the break and have other surfers in the water, it is a session like any other, with the same precautions.

Is it illegal to surf alone?

Generally, no. Some beaches have their own rules or patrol hours, so check the signs or ask the lifeguards when you get there.

How good do you need to be to surf alone?

There is no official threshold. A good benchmark: you are a strong swimmer, you can spot a rip and get out of one, and you know the break in that day's conditions. The rest is confidence, and that comes with time in the water.
